Hands On, the first run

The first time this kit ran, prepared for and presented with MNGAIA · AI4MN. Sean led it, Dave ran the breakout rooms, and Amanda led a room for anyone who couldn't open the site. So many people had already built something that the morning became builders showing each other what works.

The short version

Three people showed tools they already use at work, four breakout rooms reported back, and Dan closed with textbooks that AI builds for students who never touch an AI chat. The same moves came up in every room: just ask, let the AI interview you one question at a time, paste the error back, and go a step at a time. So did the same two cares: meet people in the tools they already use, and treat student data as part of every build. It ended on sharing: Sheets that carry their scripts, GitHub for work others can undo and build on, and offers of time from people who've done it.

Questions, answered

Asked out loud, raised in a room's report, or answered by a demo. Each answer is what the room said, tightened.

  1. The AI gave me an error, or something I don't understand. Now what?

    Room 1, reported by Amanda; Sean, after room 3 · In the recording at 1:13:41

    Tell it exactly that: "I don't know what to do with this. Break it down for me step by step." Paste the error message itself into the chat, ask what it means, and ask it to fix it. Sean said that's how many of them learned to code: an idea, an error, the error back into the chat, a fix, with a lot of copy and paste in between. When an explanation flies over your head, ask for it in smaller chunks. You learn the code and how to learn with AI at the same time.

    More: three moves for when you're stuck, on Grant a wish.

  2. How do I write the right prompt?

    Room 2, reported by Patrick; room 4, reported by Meghan · In the recording at 1:16:16

    You don't have to write one big, perfect prompt. Ask the AI to ask you questions first ("What will you need from me to make this successful?"), answer them, and keep adjusting until it makes the thing you want; the more you do it, the more you know what to say up front. Sean's version, which he gave room 1: "I've got an idea for a code project. Ask me one question at a time to help generate the code." Room 4 called it just asking: say what you're looking for and what you want to do, and ask for help.

  3. It worked, I added one more feature, and now it's broken.

    Room 3, reported by Ken · In the recording at 1:18:58

    Most builders know this one. Room 3 built the Drive inventory script from the starters, started over from scratch when it wouldn't run the way they wanted, got it working, and then broke it adding a feature. Ken's habit: ask for a step-by-step plan, do the first step, attach the file back into the chat, and ask "check my work: does this look right?" so the chat keeps you on track. Sean's: the hardest part is taking your hands off the code instead of adding one more thing. Dan's: version control, so you can always go back (below).

    More: the Drive inventory script, on Three levels.

  4. How do I put an interactive page on a Google Site without the frame around it?

    Sean · In the recording at 14:02

    Embed the HTML as a page of its own instead of as a box on a page. Sean mentioned it in passing at an earlier event, and a lot of people told him it was the useful part, because it got rid of a header they couldn't otherwise remove. Google Sites calls it a full page embed, added from the Pages tab; Google's announcement has the steps. A flat document becomes an interactive page on your site, quickly.

    More: level 2, on Three levels.

  5. How do I share a script with someone in another district?

    Sean, after Ken's walkthrough app · In the recording at 21:03

    Build it inside a Google Sheet, so the script is bound to the Sheet. Clear out the data and share the Sheet: whoever makes a copy gets the script with it, ready to run in their own Drive. Sean called it an easy, low-friction way to get classroom teachers started ("many hands make light work"), and Ken's report portal travels this way. It spreads the load, too: each copy carries only its own school's data (next question).

  6. Where do Apps Scripts start to break?

    Sean asked Paul · In the recording at 27:44

    When the data gets heavy. Paul's peer-evaluator app, where observers worked through the Danielson rubric and saved long notes into a Sheet, kept working all year but got visibly slower: the Sheet can hold it all, but pulling it back out into the web app takes longer and longer. Keeping some of it in the browser's cache helped, not enough; Paul thinks that one has outgrown Apps Script. Sean's way around it, for a tool many people use: give each group its own copy, so no one Sheet carries everyone.

    Same moment, later: In the recording at 1:27:41

  7. How do I make a tool someone else can set up on their own?

    Sean, after Ken's report portal · In the recording at 1:35:47

    Ask the AI to build a setup wizard that walks the person through it one step at a time, so nobody has to find the line in the code where the folder ID goes. A sidebar in the Sheet, like the one Kate showed, gives people menus and buttons instead of code. Then ask: "Imagine I am experiencing this for the very first time. What steps would someone need to be successful using it?" and design those steps with it. Sean: those are the very sentences he uses; it's plain language, saying what the need is, until the tool can do the work.

  8. How do we get score reports to families securely when the student information system can't?

    Ken · In the recording at 1:31:48

    Ken had to send families the actual MCA reports, one PDF per subject per student. Infinite Campus can attach them only one at a time, an add-on didn't make sense for a district his size, and mailing them takes too much time. So he built a portal in about two days with Gemini, as a script bound to a Sheet. The PDFs go in one Drive folder (each file name carries the student's MARSS number, as MCA sends them, and a built-in renamer handles the rest); guardians and their emails go in the Sheet; and the setup steps walk you through deploying it as a web app. Families get the link by email from the student information system, enter their email, receive a one-time six-digit code, and download only their own student's files. Adding a language code to the link shows the page in Spanish or Somali. He has used it for MCA and WIDA reports.

    More: the report portal, which Ken offered to share.

  9. How do I give families, staff, and the business office the privacy information each one needs?

    Room 4, reported by Meghan; Sean · In the recording at 1:23:09

    Room 4's advice was to just ask: describe the three audiences and what each needs, and ask the AI to help write the script. Sean showed where asking leads. His privacy inventory began in AI Studio with one question: can you take a privacy policy's web address and make it readable? Then one more ask at a time: measure it against federal and state law, list the data each tool uses, publish a library families can browse, add a request portal for staff, and generate a data privacy agreement to download and send the vendor. Eighteen months on, he's learning to open-source it so others can start from it.

    More: student data, on Sources.

  10. Can students learn from AI-made material without using AI themselves?

    Dan · In the recording at 1:42:02

    That's how Dan builds intelligent textbooks: AI writes the book, and students never use a language model. The pages are interactive on purpose. A picture is never just a picture: students click to hear a story or explore a part, and Dan used one AI tool to make the image and another to add the clickable layer on top. Every click is a chance to see whether a concept has landed. Dan calls these level 2, the way self-driving cars have levels; fully autonomous is years away.

    More: Dan's textbooks, in the index.

  11. How would a book know what a student understands, without a quiz?

    Dan · In the recording at 1:51:52

    Each book has a brain: a graph where every concept is a point, and the order you'd teach them is the line between two points. What a student does on the page suggests which concepts they've mastered, so a teacher can step in during week two instead of finding out at the end of the course. Dan's two cautions: that activity is student data, so where it's stored matters (Canvas keeps enrollments and exam scores, but doesn't model concepts); and none of this has yet been tested against a paper textbook, which is why he's looking for a university to study it.

  12. What comes after Apps Script?

    Dan; Sean · In the recording at 1:48:19

    Version control, first. Dan's rule: before anything else, set up the teacher's computer so everything is undoable, with GitHub. Then the AI can build boldly, you can say "I don't like everything that you did since last Tuesday" and it's undone, and students get stable versions. After that, the desktop: an AI agent (Dan named Claude, ChatGPT, Google Antigravity, Devin, and AI running on your own hardware), an editor to see what it makes (VS Code), and on Windows, the Windows Subsystem for Linux. Plan on about an hour for Windows and twenty minutes for a Mac. It helps to know the idea underneath: a big problem broken into small ones until each has a recipe. Sean is writing a guide to GitHub for teachers; for him and Paul, version control was the breakthrough.

    More: level 4, the workbench, on Three levels.

How the morning went, for anyone hosting

The plan is on Hosts. Here's what happened instead, in order.

  1. Welcome ran on the chat, as planned, with the stage's wait time doing the counting: a minute for wishes, then the 1-to-5 (mostly ones and twos, some fours; Sean suspected the twos were threes), then three minutes for the most useful thing people had built, which showed a room full of builders. Dan's network blocked the site, so a host posted the backup deck in the chat.
  2. The three levels were named, not shown. Sean skipped the live demo ("I'd rather see what some other people are doing"), and Show & Tell grew to three people: Kate, Ken, and Paul.
  3. Everyone signed up for a level in the Breakout Planner, so each room would sit in people's zone of proximal development: people who would help them learn, and people they could inspire. Amanda turned level 1 into a room for anyone who couldn't open the site, working from the starter code slides, and Dave made a room for people who hadn't signed up, to talk through how others built things.
  4. The rooms ran about half an hour. One person missed Meet's notice for their room while signing up for a GitHub account, with the notice hidden behind other tabs; moving latecomers took two hosts; and the room timer was set but not started until halfway through.
  5. As the rooms came back, Sean's camera broke, so Dave started the room reports while Sean rejoined. Then came four room reports, three more demos, and Dan's textbooks; Sean thanked him afterward "for jumping in, cold." Grant a wish, Show & Cheer, and the wonder and worry walls didn't happen, and the walls deck is still empty.
  6. Amanda had invited a cultural family advocate from her district the day before, because he'd been talking about AI. He came.
  7. Sean, at the close: "It was different than what I imagined, and I think it was great, and it was fun."

For next time: open the shared doc for anyone to edit before the day (Sean noticed it wasn't, partway through Welcome); start the room timer when the rooms open; give a level 1 room to anyone the site is blocked for; have a co-host ready to run the room reports; and when the chat shows a room full of builders, give Show & Tell the time and let the plan bend. Dave and Sean agreed that breakout rooms are not Meet's strongest feature.
