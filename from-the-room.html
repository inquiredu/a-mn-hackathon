Questions, answered

Asked out loud, asked in the chat, raised in a room's report, or answered by a demo. Open one to read what the room said.

Getting unstuck

The AI gave me an error, or something I don't understand. Now what?

Room 1, reported by Amanda; Sean, after room 3 · In the recording at 1:13:41

Tell it exactly that: "I don't know what to do with this. Break it down for me step by step." Paste the error message itself into the chat, ask what it means, and ask it to fix it. Sean said that's how many of them learned to code: an idea, an error, the error back into the chat, a fix, with a lot of copy and paste in between. When an explanation flies over your head, ask for it in smaller chunks. You learn the code and how to learn with AI at the same time.

More: three moves for when you're stuck, on Grant a wish.

How do I write the right prompt?

Room 2, reported by Patrick; room 4, reported by Meghan · In the recording at 1:16:16

You don't have to write one big, perfect prompt. Ask the AI to ask you questions first ("What will you need from me to make this successful?"), answer them, and keep adjusting until it makes the thing you want; the more you do it, the more you know what to say up front. Sean's version, which he gave room 1: "I've got an idea for a code project. Ask me one question at a time to help generate the code." Room 4 called it just asking: say what you're looking for and what you want to do, and ask for help.

It worked, I added one more feature, and now it's broken.

Room 3, reported by Ken · In the recording at 1:18:58

Most builders know this one. Room 3 built the Drive inventory script from the starters, started over from scratch when it wouldn't run the way they wanted, got it working, and then broke it adding a feature. Ken's habit: ask for a step-by-step plan, do the first step, attach the file back into the chat, and ask "check my work: does this look right?" so the chat keeps you on track. Sean's: the hardest part is taking your hands off the code instead of adding one more thing. Dan's: version control, so you can always go back (below).

Building and sharing

How do I put an interactive page on a Google Site without the frame around it?

Sean · In the recording at 14:02

Embed the HTML as a page of its own instead of as a box on a page. Sean mentioned it in passing at an earlier event, and a lot of people told him it was the useful part, because it got rid of a header they couldn't otherwise remove. Google Sites calls it a full page embed, added from the Pages tab; Google's announcement has the steps. A flat document becomes an interactive page on your site, quickly.

How do I share a script with someone in another district?

Sean, after Ken's walkthrough app · In the recording at 21:03

Build it inside a Google Sheet, so the script is bound to the Sheet. Clear out the data and share the Sheet: whoever makes a copy gets the script with it, ready to run in their own Drive. Sean called it an easy, low-friction way to get classroom teachers started ("many hands make light work"), and Ken's report portal travels this way. It spreads the load, too: each copy carries only its own school's data (next question).

How do I make a tool someone else can set up on their own?

Sean, after Ken's report portal · In the recording at 1:35:47

Ask the AI to build a setup wizard that walks the person through it one step at a time, so nobody has to find the line in the code where the folder ID goes. A sidebar in the Sheet, like the one Kate showed, gives people menus and buttons instead of code. Then ask: "Imagine I am experiencing this for the very first time. What steps would someone need to be successful using it?" and design those steps with it. Sean: those are the very sentences he uses; it's plain language, saying what the need is, until the tool can do the work.

Sean asked Paul · In the recording at 27:44

When the data gets heavy. How do we get score reports to families securely when the student information system can't?

Ken · In the recording at 1:31:48

As his district's assessment coordinator, Ken had to send families the actual MCA reports, one PDF per subject per student, in a way that keeps to FERPA. Infinite Campus can attach them only one at a time, an add-on didn't make sense for a district his size, and mailing them takes too much time. So he built a portal in about two days with Gemini, as a script bound to a Sheet. The PDFs go in one Drive folder (each file name carries the student's MARSS number, as MCA sends them, and a built-in renamer handles the rest); guardians and their emails go in the Sheet; and the setup steps walk you through deploying it as a web app. Families get the link by email from the student information system, enter their email, receive a one-time six-digit code, and download only their own student's files. Adding a language code to the link shows the page in Spanish or Somali. He has used it for MCA and ACCESS (WIDA) reports.

Going further

Can students learn from AI-made material without using AI themselves?

Dan · In the recording at 1:42:02

That's how Dan builds intelligent textbooks: AI writes the book, and students never use a language model. The pages are interactive on purpose. A picture is never just a picture: students click to hear a story or explore a part, and Dan used one AI tool to make the image and another to add the clickable layer on top. Every click is a chance to see whether a concept has landed. Dan calls these level 2, the way self-driving cars have levels; fully autonomous is years away.

More: Dan's textbooks, in the index.

How would a book know what a student understands, without a quiz?

Dan · In the recording at 1:51:52

Each book has a brain: a graph where every concept is a point, and the order you'd teach them is the line between two points. What a student does on the page suggests which concepts they've mastered, so a teacher can step in during week two instead of finding out at the end of the course. Dan's two cautions: that activity is student data, so where it's stored matters (Canvas keeps enrollments and exam scores, but doesn't model concepts); and none of this has yet been tested against a paper textbook, which is why he's looking for a university to study it.

What comes after Apps Script?

Dan; Sean · In the recording at 1:48:19

Version control, first. Dan's rule: before anything else, set up the teacher's computer so everything is undoable, with GitHub. Then the AI can build boldly, you can say "I don't like everything that you did since last Tuesday" and it's undone, and students get stable versions. After that, the desktop: an AI agent (Dan named Claude, ChatGPT, Google Antigravity, Devin, and AI running on your own hardware), an editor to see what it makes (VS Code), and on Windows, the Windows Subsystem for Linux. Plan on about an hour for Windows and twenty minutes for a Mac. It helps to know the idea underneath: a big problem broken into small ones until each has a recipe. Sean is writing a guide to GitHub for teachers; for him and Paul, version control was the breakthrough.

Researched after the session

Could an event check-in app help reunify families after an emergency evacuation?Researched

Nathaniel, in the chat, after Kate's and Paul's check-in tools. Not answered on the day.

It could help with one piece: a running record of which students are where, and when each one was released. It shouldn't be what decides who a child goes home with. In the Standard Reunification Method, which many districts use, students wait apart from arriving parents; each parent fills out a reunification card, staff check photo ID and custody, a runner brings the student, and a final step accounts for every child. Federal guidance says a plan should verify that an adult is authorized to take custody. The method runs on paper, and reunification can follow a power outage, so a plan that uses an app still needs a paper way it has practiced. Minnesota requires every school board to adopt a crisis management policy, written with law enforcement and emergency managers; the statute lists drills but doesn't mention reunification. Your district's crisis team decides what a tool may do, not the person who built it.

Is a hidden or protected sheet a safe place for student data behind a web app?Researched

Behind Patrick's follow-up to Paul in the chat, which went unanswered.

No: hiding and protecting keep a Sheet tidy; they don't lock it. Google's help says protection shouldn't be relied on for security: anyone with access can still print, copy, and export the file, and every editor can unhide a hidden sheet. The real gate is who the spreadsheet is shared with. A web app set to run as you (Execute as: Me) uses your access, so staff can use the app without being shared on the spreadsheet; the app's own code then decides what each person sees, and the deployment's Who has access setting decides who can open it at all. Inside your district's Google domain the script can usually tell who is visiting; for other domains or personal accounts it may get a blank. FERPA asks for reasonable methods so that staff reach only the records they have a legitimate educational interest in. Your district's data privacy lead decides what that means for a given tool.

  • Web apps: who the script runs as, and who can open it (Apps Script)
  • Session: when a script can and can't see who is visiting (Apps Script)
  • Legitimate educational interest (U.S. Department of Education)
What limits do Apps Script and Google Sheets actually have?Researched

Behind Sean's question to Paul about where scripts break.

Each run of a script gets six minutes; a custom function used in a cell gets thirty seconds. Daily allowances are per person, and a Google Workspace account (school accounts are Workspace accounts) gets more than a personal one: 1,500 email recipients a day instead of 100, and six hours of trigger time a day instead of ninety minutes. One person can run thirty scripts at once. Since September 2026 a spreadsheet can hold 20 million cells, up from 10 million, but a Sheet slows down long before it's full. Google's advice is to read a whole range in one call, work on it, and write it back in one call, rather than cell by cell. Apps Script's own cache holds up to 100KB per item for at most six hours, and may let go sooner. Google says quotas can change, so check its page before planning around a number.

What this rests on, checked October 12, 2026:

  • Quotas for Google services (Apps Script)
  • Best practices: batch reads and writes (Apps Script)
  • Doubled cell limits in Google Sheets (Google Workspace Updates, September 2026)
  • Cache limits (Apps Script)
What comes along when someone copies a Sheet that has a script?Researched

Behind Sean's "many hands make light work," and Ken's portal, which travels this way.

The script does. Whoever makes the copy owns it and gets their own copy of the script, and their changes never touch yours. What's tied to a person stays behind. Installable triggers, such as a timed run or a run when a form is submitted, belong to whoever made them, so the new owner sets them up again. A web app is a deployment, so the copy has none until its owner deploys it, and then it has its own address. The first time the new owner runs it, Google asks them to approve what it can reach. A share link ending in /copy instead of /edit opens a Make a copy prompt. Before sharing, clear out the data and remove any keys or settings stored with the script. A district can turn Apps Script off or limit sharing outside the district, so a copy that runs for you may not run for a teacher in another district.

What this rests on, checked October 12, 2026:

  • Container-bound scripts (Apps Script)
  • Collaborating: triggers aren't shared (Apps Script)
  • Deployments (Apps Script)
  • Share a copy link (Google Workspace Learning Center)
  • Turning Apps Script on or off (Google Workspace Admin Help)
Can an Apps Script project use version control, like GitHub?Researched

Behind Dan's advice to set up version control first, and Sean's guide to GitHub for teachers.

Yes, two ways. In the Apps Script editor, Project History keeps the versions you create, usually one each time you deploy, up to 200, and can highlight what changed. It isn't a record of every save, and going back means copying the old code in by hand. For full version control, Google's free tool clasp copies a project's files to your computer, where they can live in a Git repository on GitHub. clasp pull brings the online code down; clasp push sends yours up and replaces what's there, so edit in one place at a time. It needs Node.js 20 or later, which usually takes administrator rights to install, and the Apps Script API turned on in your Apps Script settings. On a district laptop that may be a request to your technology office: it's level 4, the workbench.

What does FERPA ask of a school that sends score reports to families electronically?Researched

Behind Ken's secure report portal.

Parents have a right to see their child's education records. Before a school discloses records by any route, FERPA asks it to use reasonable methods to identify and authenticate the person receiving them. The rule doesn't name a method. Federal guidance asks for certainty that fits how sensitive the data is, and describes the factors: something you know, something you have, something you are. A one-time code sent to an email address is one such check; how much it proves depends on where the address came from, who else can read that inbox, and whether a family can reach only its own student's reports. Either parent keeps full rights unless a court order or similar legal document says otherwise. In Minnesota, the state treats these reports as private student data, and districts keep them secure until they reach families. The district chooses its method and answers for it; this isn't a ruling on any one tool.

What this rests on, checked October 12, 2026:

  • 34 CFR 99.31, including (c), authenticating identity (eCFR)
  • Identity authentication best practices (U.S. Department of Education)
  • 34 CFR 99.4, the rights of parents
  • Assessment Update, September 30, 2026 (Minnesota Department of Education)
What does Minnesota's law ask districts to tell families about technology providers?Researched

Behind Meghan's three audiences and Sean's privacy inventory.

Since the 2022-23 school year, Minnesota's educational data law, section 13.32, has rules for technology providers that handle student data under a school contract. That data isn't the provider's property. The provider can't sell or share it or use it commercially, must tell the school about a breach, and must return or destroy the data within 90 days after the contract ends unless renewal is expected. Within 30 days of the start of each school year, a district must give families direct notice, by mail, email, or something like them, of each curriculum, testing, or assessment technology provider with access to student data, which data, and whom to ask; families can inspect the contracts. A separate part limits remote monitoring of school-issued devices (location, camera, microphone, keystrokes, browsing) to listed exceptions. That covers the families; staff and the business office are the district's to decide, with its data practices official.

More: 13.32, on Sources.

Can learning be measured from what students do, instead of from quizzes?Researched

Behind Dan's intelligent textbooks, and ahead of the November session on assessment.

Partly, and researchers have worked on it for decades. Since Corbett and Anderson's 1994 paper, intelligent tutors have used knowledge tracing: each step a student takes updates the tutor's estimate of whether they know a skill. Valerie Shute's stealth assessment builds the measuring into a game or task, so students show what they know by doing it. Both rest on careful design: a map of the skills, much like a concept graph; a plan for which actions count as evidence of each one; and studies showing the estimates agree with other trusted measures. Clicks count only when the task actually requires the skill, and these models can carry bias. One correction to the record: Canvas does track mastery, through Outcomes and the Learning Mastery Gradebook, scored from rubrics and assessments a teacher aligns to standards. What it doesn't do is infer mastery from page activity, which is the gap Dan described.

How can a host make Google Meet breakout rooms run more smoothly?Researched

Behind the morning's breakout rooms: a missed notice, latecomers to move, and two hosts agreeing they're not Meet's strongest feature.

Do most of the setup before the meeting. Check your edition first: Google's help lists Education Plus and the Teaching and Learning Upgrade among those that can create rooms, so not every school account can. A host can build the rooms in the Google Calendar event and pre-assign people by typing names, dragging, or shuffling. Turn on Host Management and add co-hosts beforehand: its settings can't change once rooms are live, and people can be made co-hosts only from the main room. Each participant gets a prompt on screen and must click Join; anyone who cancels stays in the main room, so say out loud and in the chat that rooms are opening, and ask everyone to bring the Meet tab to the front. Set the timer, which every room can see. Hosts can join any room, and a room can ask for help.

Every room landed here on its own. Say what you need, let the AI ask you questions one at a time, paste the error back, and go a step at a time; ask for smaller chunks when an answer flies past you. The limit is often one we put on ourselves, and the moves carry beyond code: they're how anyone learns with AI.

Meet people where they already work

The tools that stuck live inside something people already open: a Sheet teachers already use, an email from a system families already know, a page in the format they need, whether that's read-aloud, Spanish or Somali, or a dictionary without the ads. One good experience in a familiar place opens the door to the next, and the surest sign a tool works is that nobody mentions it anymore.

Start small, and know the edge

Each level seems impossible until it works, and then the next one does too. A walkthrough app took twenty minutes; a year of observation notes slowed a Sheet until it dragged. Know where a script stops, keep versions you can undo, and expect the ceiling to keep moving, remembering that all of it is new and still untested.

How these records are made

Each record is written from the recording's transcript and the meeting's chat, checked line by line. People appear by first name, and only for what they showed or said to the whole room, out loud or in the chat; side conversations stay out. Links are the ones people posted; an entry without one says so. A time like 27:44 marks the moment in the recording, which the hosts are sending to everyone who was there, and in the transcript, which is in Gemini's notes for the meeting (AI4MN's own doc). Where Gemini's summary and the transcript differ, the record follows the transcript. The chat's own times run a minute or two apart from the recording's, so chat posts are marked "in the chat" instead.
