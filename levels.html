Three levels of getting started with code

Three ways to build something real with AI, and three steps toward feeling at home with code. At none of them do you write code from scratch: you copy, paste, ask for changes, and look at what comes back. Each level puts code somewhere new, so each one shows you something new about what code is and does. Every level is a good level. The best one is the one that would stretch you a little.

At any level: three habits

You don't need to write code to start learning it. You need to see it, change it, and notice what changed.

Guess first

Before you press Play or Run, say what you think will happen, then check. Predicting is the first step of PRIMM, a way of teaching programming the research on Sources supports.

Look inside

Open the code and find one thing you recognize: a word you saw on the screen, a number you could change. The rest can stay a mystery for now.

Change one thing

One request at a time, then say what changed. When it breaks, describe what's on the screen in plain words. That's what programmers do too.

In the chat

Twenty minutes · an AI chat

A small tool that runs right inside your AI chat: a timer, a picker, a quiz, a fortune-teller. You describe it, the AI writes it, and it appears in a panel beside the conversation, working, with buttons you can press. Nothing to install and nothing to publish.

How it works

When you ask for a tool, the AI writes a small web page: one file of code. Three kinds of code share that file. HTML says what's on the page: a heading, a button, a list. CSS says how it looks: colors, sizes, spacing. JavaScript says what happens when you press something: the countdown, the shuffle, the answer. Gemini's Canvas and Claude's artifacts run that file in a panel beside the conversation; ChatGPT's Preview runs it in place of the code. Either way the conversation keeps it: open the chat again and it's still there.

Why start here

It's the shortest loop there is: ask, look, change, look again, in seconds. Nothing to install, nothing to publish, nobody else sees it until you share it, and the tool can't reach your files.

What it teaches you about code

  • Code is text. A whole working tool is one file you can scroll from top to bottom. Ask your AI to show you the code, and scroll it once.
  • Find what you can see. On Starters, press Play, then Show the code. Find a word that's on the screen, change it, and press Run my changes.
  • Name the parts. Ask: "Which part of this is the HTML, the CSS, and the JavaScript? Show me the line that makes the button work."
  • Ask what changed. After each change: "Which lines did you change, and why?"

An example

The Teacher's Lounge Oracle from the Starters page, pasted into a chat, answering questions; then taught a new topic with one request.

Try it

  1. Open the starters and press Play on one. Guess what it does first.
  2. Press Copy for my AI. It copies the tool's whole code with a short note asking the AI to run it. Paste it into your AI chat (where, in each tool) and send.
  3. Ask for one change. Then another. Then break it on purpose: ask for something it can't quite do ("make it work with 500 names"), and when it goes wrong, describe what's on the screen in plain words.

On your site

An hour · a Google Site you can edit

A document you already have becomes an interactive page, and you put it on your Google Site with a feature most people never find: Embed code, which places the page you built inside a box on your site. It looks like part of the site. It is the thing you built.

How it works

It's the same kind of file as level 1: a web page in one file of code. What changes is where it lives. Embed code puts that file in a box on a page of your Google Site, where anyone your Site is shared with can open it. Google keeps the box sealed off from the rest of your site. That keeps your site safe, and it's why the page can't save anything between visits.

Why this level

Your document already has the words; the AI gives them a shape. And it's the first time other people use what you built: your team, families, students.

What it teaches you about code

  • The place sets the rules. The same code runs in a chat and on your Site, but on a Site it can't save between visits or reach your visitors' accounts. When something works in the chat and not on the Site, the place is usually why.
  • Words and looks are separate. Ask for a change to the look only ("larger type, navy cards") and watch your words stay put. Then change only the words.
  • Find where things live. Ask: "Where in the code are the colors set? Where does the text of each card live?"
  • Who sees it is a setting. Preview is just you; Publish is whoever your Site is shared with. The code is the same either way.

An example

A department charter goes into Gemini with Canvas on, comes out as an interactive page of cards and badges, and lands on a Google Site in about a minute. Two finished examples are on the Starters page: the Charter Cards and the Syllabus Page.

Before you start

  • A document to turn into a page: a charter, a syllabus, a handbook page, the norms on the wall. Made-up or your own; nothing about students. A poster? Type its words into a Doc first. A PDF? Open it and copy the text.
  • A Google Site you can edit. No Site yet? Go to sites.google.com, choose Blank site, and give it a name. You don't have to publish it to try this: Preview shows it working.
Where do I paste it, and what you'll see

Try it

  1. Copy the prompt. In your AI chat, switch on Canvas (Gemini) or get ready to press Preview (ChatGPT), as in Where do I paste it, then paste the prompt. Don't send yet.
    Read the prompt

    Here is a document my team already uses. Turn it into an interactive infographic as a single HTML page: each section becomes a card that opens when clicked or tapped, the key commitments become a row of badges, in plain language and large type, and it works on a phone. Keep the document's meaning exactly; don't add anything it doesn't say. No outside libraries, images, or fonts. The document is below.

  2. Open your document, select all of its text (Ctrl + A, or ⌘ + A on a Mac), copy it, and paste it under the prompt in the same message. Send.
  3. When the page looks right, copy its code. The code is the text that makes the page work; it starts with <!doctype html>. Each tool has its own copy spot (listed above). Copy all of it, first line to last.
  4. Open your Google Site to edit it. Choose Insert, then Embed, then the Embed code tab. Paste, choose Next, then Insert.
  5. Drag the box's corner until the whole page shows. Preview shows it to you; Publish shows it to whoever your Site is shared with, and Sites asks you who that is.

You'll know it worked

Your document, as cards you can open, inside a box on your Site's page in Preview.

If it breaks

  • Sites says the code is too long: ask your AI for a lighter version, fewer sections and less decoration.
  • The box stays blank after you publish: your district may limit embeds. Ask your technology office.

Behind the scenes

An hour the first time, fifteen minutes after that · your Google Drive

A short script does a job for you inside Google Workspace. A script is a few instructions Google runs for you, in a tool called Apps Script that's built into every Google account; you approve what it may touch before it runs. No screen to look at, just work that gets done. The AI writes it, you paste it, you press Run.

How it works

Apps Script is JavaScript, the same language that makes level 1's buttons work, but it runs on Google's computers instead of in your browser, signed in as you. There's no page to look at. Instead it can open a folder, read every file in it, and make a Sheet. You paste the script into a project and press Run, and the first time, Google asks what it may touch. Below the code, the Execution log is where the script tells you what it did, or what went wrong.

Why this level

It's the first code that does a job instead of showing you something: work you'd do by hand, done in seconds, every time you press Run. That's also why it has a gate: a script can do whatever you can do in your Drive.

What it teaches you about code

  • A function is a named set of steps. Ours is called inventory: it's the name you choose before you press Run.
  • Settings sit at the top. FOLDER_ID is the one line you change, so nothing else needs touching. Ask your AI why scripts are written that way.
  • Make it talk. Logger.log writes a line to the Execution log. Ask your AI to add one ("log each folder's name as you go") and watch the script narrate its work.
  • Errors are messages. Red text in the log says what went wrong and where. Paste it into your AI with "What went wrong?"
  • Permissions come from the code. Google asks for Drive and Sheets because the script uses them. A script that asks for more than its job needs is asking too much.

An example

A Drive folder with folders inside it, and a script that reads all of them and writes a Sheet: every document, its type, and a link to open it. A whole curriculum, one search away.

A script acts with your account

  • Whatever you can open, it can open. So read it before you run it, or have it read to you: Copy for my AI, below, hands it to your AI with a note asking for a line-by-line explanation. Ours is short enough to read on this page. A line nobody can explain doesn't run.
  • The permission screen is the gate. It names exactly what the script may touch. Read it like a permission slip. Ours asks for Drive and Sheets, because that's its whole job; a script that asks for Gmail to list a folder is asking for too much.
  • Practice files while you're learning. Point it at a folder of made-up documents. Nothing about real students goes into a script you can't yet explain.
  • A script that arrives with no explanation stays unrun, and never share one that touches student data. If script.google.com won't open, your district turned it off, and that's a conversation with your technology director, not a workaround.
