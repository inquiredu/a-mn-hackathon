Sources

How-tos that take the mystery out of building with AI, cautions worth knowing, and what research says about learning to code. Every source here was opened and checked on October 7, 2026. Product help pages change often, so their dates are noted.

Learn the moves Proceed with care Coding and thinking

The short version

Learning to code reliably builds coding skill, and a large meta-analysis found moderate links to creative thinking, math, and metacognition, though those links shrink when compared with other active teaching. So "coding makes you smarter" says more than the research does. The research is stronger on how to begin: run something that works, change it, then make your own. Early studies of AI and beginners suggest AI help need not hurt learning when people still change code by hand and read what the AI made before keeping it. The main risks are over-reliance, confident mistakes, and data going places it shouldn't, and each has a simple habit that answers it.

How Friday is built on this

The morning follows a path researchers call Use-Modify-Create (Lee and colleagues, 2011), later developed into PRIMM: predict, run, investigate, modify, make (Sentance and colleagues, 2019). You start with a starter that works, guess what it does, run it, look inside, change it by hand, remix it with AI, and then build your own wish. Brennan and Resnick (2012) name "reusing and remixing" as a core practice of computational thinking, and their suggestions for assessing it lead straight into next month's conversation.

Vibe coding, named and bounded

  • The post that named "vibe coding"

    Andrej Karpathy, on X · February 2, 2025 Primary source

    Karpathy describes giving in to the AI so fully that you stop looking at the code, accepting its changes and pasting errors back in. He calls it fine for throwaway weekend projects.

    For Friday: names what we'll be doing, and its limits, in the words of the person who coined it.

  • Not all AI-assisted programming is vibe coding (but vibe coding rocks)

    Simon Willison · March 19, 2025 Essay

    Willison separates vibe coding, building without reviewing the code, from AI-assisted programming where you could explain every line. His advice: vibe code low-stakes projects, and ask someone experienced to check anything other people will rely on. He also calls it an eye-opening way for beginners to learn.

    For Friday: one simple rule for what to build and what to share.

  • Here's how I use LLMs to help me write code

    Simon Willison · March 11, 2025 Essay

    Practical habits from an experienced programmer: give the model context, tell it exactly what you want, test everything it writes, and expect several rounds of revision.

    For Friday: try it, test it, ask again. That's the whole loop.

Proceed with care

Each caution comes with a habit that answers it. None of them is a reason not to build. All of them are reasons to build thoughtfully.

When AI writes the code

  • Do users write more insecure code with AI assistants?

    Perry, Srivastava, Kumar & Boneh · ACM CCS 2023 Peer-reviewed

    In a study of 47 participants, those using an AI assistant wrote less secure code and were more likely to believe it was secure. Participants who trusted the AI less and reworked their prompts did better.

    The habit: treat the AI's confidence as a claim to check. Ask it what could go wrong with your tool and where any typed information goes.

  • Asleep at the keyboard? Assessing the security of GitHub Copilot's code contributions

    Pearce, Ahmad, Tan, Dolan-Gavitt & Karri · IEEE Symposium on Security and Privacy 2022 Peer-reviewed

    Across 89 scenarios built around high-risk weaknesses, the researchers judged about 40% of 1,689 generated programs to be vulnerable. They tested a 2021 tool on security-sensitive tasks, so this isn't a general error rate.

    The habit: keep Friday's tools simple: no logins, no passwords, no storing other people's information.

  • We have a package for you! A comprehensive analysis of package hallucinations by code generating LLMs

    Spracklen and colleagues · USENIX Security 2025 Peer-reviewed

    Across 16 models and 576,000 code samples, models invented software packages that don't exist, on average at least 5.2% of the time for commercial models and 21.7% for open-source models.

    The habit: build tools that run right in the AI's preview. If the AI tells you to install something, check with your IT team first.

Student data

  • Protecting student privacy while using online educational services

    U.S. Department of Education, Privacy Technical Assistance Center · 2014 Federal guidance

    As a general rule, schools can't share personal information from education records with a provider without parent consent unless a FERPA exception applies. Clicking "accept" on terms of service is like signing a contract, and free tools should go through the same approval as paid ones. It predates generative AI and is still posted as current guidance.

    The habit: know your district's approval process before real student information goes into any tool.

  • Vetting generative AI tools for use in schools

    David Sallay, Future of Privacy Forum · 2024 Policy brief

    A student ID number or a school email address still counts as personal information under FERPA and many state laws. Schools should ask whether a vendor uses student information to train its models.

    The habit: use made-up names and made-up data. Initials and ID numbers don't make data anonymous.

  • Minnesota Statutes § 13.32, educational data

    Minnesota Office of the Revisor of Statutes · amended by Laws 2022, chapter 69 Law

    The 2022 amendments say educational data held by a technology provider isn't the provider's property, can't be used commercially, and must be returned or destroyed within 90 days after a contract ends. Schools must tell parents which providers have access within 30 days of the start of the school year. The law defines technology providers around contracts for school-issued devices, so whether it reaches a particular AI tool is a question for your district.

    The habit: ask your district's data practices contact before any tool touches real student records.

  • Artificial intelligence in education

    Minnesota Department of Education · as of October 2026 State guidance

    Asks educators how data is collected and used and whether a tool meets data practices standards, names over-reliance as a challenge, and names designing and innovating with AI as an opportunity.

    The habit: run your tool idea through MDE's questions before you share it.

Accessibility of what you share

  • Fact sheet: new rule on the accessibility of web content and mobile apps

    U.S. Department of Justice, ADA.gov · rule published 2024, dates extended April 2026 Federal rule

    State and local governments, including school districts, must meet WCAG 2.1 Level AA for their web content and apps. An interim final rule on April 20, 2026 moved the compliance dates to April 26, 2027 for entities serving 50,000 people or more, and April 26, 2028 for smaller ones and special districts. A lawsuit challenging the extension was filed in May 2026, according to a law firm's summary; we found no ruling as of October 7.

    The habit: before a tool goes on a public district page, check that it works by keyboard, has readable contrast, and labels its controls.

  • Easy checks: a first review of web accessibility

    W3C Web Accessibility Initiative, with WCAG 2.1 · as of October 2026 Standard and checklist

    WCAG is organized around four principles: perceivable, operable, understandable, and robust. Easy Checks is a quick first look, and W3C notes that a page can pass it and still have real barriers.

    The habit: ask your AI to make the tool meet WCAG 2.1 AA, then run Easy Checks and try it with only a keyboard.

Learning and over-reliance

  • Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: evidence from high school mathematics

    Bastani, Bastani, Sungu, Ge, Kabakcı & Mariman · PNAS 2025 Peer-reviewed

    In a field experiment with nearly 1,000 high school students, access to GPT-4 raised practice scores (by 48% with a standard chat, 127% with a tutor version built with guardrails). When access was taken away, the standard-chat group did 17% worse than students who never had it. The guardrails largely prevented that harm.

    The habit: if you build something for students, have it give hints and questions, not answers.

  • The impact of generative AI on critical thinking

    Lee, Sarkar, Tankelevitch, Drosos, Rintel, Banks & Wilson · CHI 2025 Peer-reviewed

    In a survey of 319 knowledge workers describing 936 tasks, higher confidence in AI went with less critical thinking. The data are self-reported and show an association, not a cause.

    The habit: build in a check step. Click every button and confirm every fact your tool shows.

  • The widening gap: the benefits and harms of generative AI for novice programmers

    Prather and colleagues · ICER 2024 Peer-reviewed

    Across 21 lab sessions, students who struggled often believed they had done better than they had, leaving with an illusion of competence.

    The habit: explain your tool back in your own words. If you can't, ask the AI to walk you through it.

A note on a widely shared study: "Your Brain on ChatGPT" (Kosmyna and colleagues, MIT Media Lab, 2025) is an arXiv preprint that hadn't been peer-reviewed as of its December 2025 version. It had 54 participants, 18 of whom completed the fourth session. Read it as early and unreviewed.

Coding and thinking

What learning to code does and doesn't do for thinking, where the ideas behind Friday come from, and what early research says about AI and beginners.

Does learning to code help thinking?

  • The cognitive benefits of learning computer programming: a meta-analysis of transfer effects

    Scherer, Siddiq & Sánchez Viveros · Journal of Educational Psychology, 2019 Peer-reviewed

    Across 105 studies and 539 effect sizes, learning to program had a moderate overall effect (g = 0.49): strong on programming itself (g = 0.75) and moderate on other thinking skills (g = 0.47). Gains were strongest for creative thinking, math, and metacognition, then spatial skills and reasoning, and smallest for school achievement and literacy. Compared with other active training, the effect was g = 0.16; compared with no training, g = 0.65.

    For Friday: call it problem-solving practice, not a promise that coding makes people smarter.

  • Remaining trouble spots with computational thinking

    Peter Denning · Communications of the ACM, 2017 Commentary

    Denning argues that claims of computational thinking helping everyone, beyond people who design computing systems, remain unsupported, and that research hasn't shown general transfer. He wrote before the 2019 meta-analysis above, which found some transfer, though weaker against active comparisons.

    For Friday: a reason to keep our claims modest.

  • A meta-analysis of teaching and learning computer programming: effective instructional approaches and conditions

    Scherer, Siddiq & Sánchez Viveros · Computers in Human Behavior, 2020 Peer-reviewed

    Across 139 interventions, programming instruction had a strong effect (g = 0.81). Teaching approaches differed only a little from each other, with collaboration and visual programming standing out.

    For Friday: no one method wins, so the design can choose for agency and collaboration: teams of four, an anchor, and building along.

Where the ideas come from

  • Computational thinking

    Jeannette Wing · Communications of the ACM, 2006 Essay

    Wing argues that computational thinking, built on abstraction and breaking problems down, is a basic skill for everyone. It's about how people conceptualize problems, not about programming, and it's a way humans think, not a way of making people think like computers.

    For Friday: the thinking stays human, even when AI writes the code.

  • Mindstorms: children, computers, and powerful ideas

    Seymour Papert · Basic Books, 1980 Book

    Drawing on children's work with the Logo programming language, Papert argues that children can master computers, and that ideas like debugging can change how people learn in general. It's vision and theory rather than controlled evidence; Grover and Pea credit it with pioneering the idea that children build procedural thinking through programming.

    For Friday: building your own tool comes from this tradition.

  • New frameworks for studying and assessing the development of computational thinking

    Karen Brennan & Mitchel Resnick · AERA Annual Meeting, 2012 Conference paper

    Computational thinking has three parts: concepts (like loops, events, and conditions), practices (like testing and debugging, and reusing and remixing), and perspectives (like expressing and questioning). For assessing it, they suggest making assessment useful to the learner, including what learners made, showing the process, checking in more than once, valuing different ways of knowing, and including several viewpoints.

    For Friday and November: remixing is a named practice, and these assessment suggestions lead straight into next month.

  • Computational thinking in K–12: a review of the state of the field

    Shuchi Grover & Roy Pea · Educational Researcher, 2013 Peer-reviewed review

    A review recommending tools with a low floor and a high ceiling, plus scaffolding. It flags open questions about transfer to other subjects and how to measure what learners gain.

    For Friday: the starters are the low floor; granting a wish from scratch is the high ceiling.

How to start: run, change, make

  • Computational thinking for youth in practice

    Lee, Martin, Denner, Coulter, Allan, Erickson, Malyn-Smith & Werner · ACM Inroads, 2011 Framework from practice

    Proposes Use-Modify-Create: learners use someone else's program, change it (first how it looks, then how it behaves) until it becomes their own, and then create their own. The authors note that learners move back and forth between stages.

    For Friday: play a starter, change it, remix it, grant a wish. That's Use-Modify-Create.

  • Teaching computer programming with PRIMM: a sociocultural perspective

    Sentance, Waite & Kallia · Computer Science Education, 2019, with a Raspberry Pi Foundation summary Peer-reviewed

    PRIMM stands for predict, run, investigate, modify, make. In 13 schools with 493 students aged 11 to 14, the PRIMM group scored higher on a post-test than a comparison group, in a quasi-experimental design. Its principles include reading code before writing it, talking about code together, and starting from working programs.

    For Friday: guess what a starter does before you press Play, and talk about the code with your team.

AI and beginners

  • Studying the effect of AI code generators on supporting novice learners in introductory programming

    Kazemitabaar, Chow, Ma, Ericson, Weintrop & Grossman · CHI 2023 Peer-reviewed

    In a controlled study of 69 novices aged 10 to 17, AI access raised code-writing performance (1.15 times the completion, 1.8 times the scores) without hurting later tasks where learners changed code by hand. Learners with more prior knowledge gained most, and in 49% of tasks where they used the AI, they submitted its code unchanged.

    For Friday: mix AI remixes with changes you make by hand, and read what the AI made before you keep it.

  • The robots are here: navigating the generative AI revolution in computing education

    Prather, Denny, Leinonen, Becker and colleagues · ITiCSE Working Group Reports, 2023 Peer-reviewed report

    Drawing on a review of 71 articles, a survey of 171 students and 57 instructors in 20 countries, and 22 interviews, the authors found over-reliance was the most common concern. Some educators are shifting to assess process: work submitted in stages, reflections, interviews, portfolios, and presentations.

    For November: a direct bridge to next month's conversation about assessment.

  • Computing education in the era of generative AI

    Denny, Prather, Becker and colleagues · Communications of the ACM, 2024 Peer-reviewed

    AI raises concerns about over-reliance and misuse, and it can also explain code and tailor practice. The authors point to breaking problems down and describing a task precisely as the skills that matter more now, and warn that leaning too hard on code generators may weaken metacognition.

    For Friday: describing what you want clearly enough for an AI to build it is the thinking skill.
